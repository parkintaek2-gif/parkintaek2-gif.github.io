Last night we counted how often Korean front pages put a person's **age** in the headline, in quote
marks, before the name. The answer was
[one entertainment desk and almost nobody else](/article/the-age-in-the-headline-is-one-desk-not-a-country):
TenAsia did it in 27.4 percent of its headlines and the business and general papers did it zero
times in 1,080.

Reading the same file again, a second habit was hard to miss — the money. "1 billion won in
damages." "A 100-million-won flex." "A 4-million-won car rebuilt for 6 million." So we counted that
too, on exactly the same 1,365 headlines.

## Each desk has one number

| Desk | Beat | Headlines | With a won amount | With a quoted age | With both |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Maeil Business | business | 695 | **155 (22.3%)** | 0 (0.0%) | **0** |
| Dong-A Ilbo | general | 385 | 45 (11.7%) | 0 (0.0%) | **0** |
| TenAsia | K-culture | 73 | 8 (11.0%) | **20 (27.4%)** | **0** |
| Star News | K-culture | 212 | 7 (3.3%) | 6 (2.8%) | **0** |

A business paper quoting money is not a finding. What the pairing shows is that the two habits are
**complementary and non-overlapping**: the desk that leads with ages almost never leads with money,
the desks that lead with money never lead with ages, and across all 1,365 headlines **not one carried
both**.

TenAsia is the interesting row. It is the only desk that does both at any real rate — a quoted age in
more than one headline in four, a won amount in one in nine — and even there the two never landed in
the same headline. Whatever selects for one appears to exclude the other within a single line of copy.

## The money figure is a floor, and here is the band

Korean headlines drop the word for won constantly. A headline reading *10-eok damages* is one billion
won in damages to any Korean reader — *eok* being the unit for a hundred million — and our pattern
does not count it, because the same pattern also catches *10-eok views*.

So we counted that set separately rather than deciding for it:

| Desk | Money (won stated) | Unit only, won omitted | Band |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Maeil Business | 155 (22.3%) | 165 | 22.3–46.0% |
| Dong-A Ilbo | 45 (11.7%) | 62 | 11.7–27.8% |
| TenAsia | 8 (11.0%) | 9 | 11.0–23.3% |
| Star News | 7 (3.3%) | 17 | 3.3–11.3% |

The low end counts only what is unambiguously money. The high end assumes every bare unit is money,
which it is not. The true figure sits between, and the ordering of the four desks is the same at both
ends — which is the part the argument above actually rests on.

## What the zero is and is not

Zero co-occurrences in 1,365 headlines is not proof that a Korean desk cannot write "'82-year-old'
Kim, 300 million won." It is a statement about a small sample: a habit that showed up once in a few
thousand headlines would read as zero here too.

What it does rule out is the casual assumption that these are both just "numbers in headlines."
They are two different tools, held by different desks, and one of the two — the age — is the one
English-language readers find strangest about Korean coverage. It is also the one confined to a
single desk.

One note on the unit above, because it changes the reading: *eok* is a hundred million, so a headline
saying *10-eok* is one billion won, not ten. English coverage of Korean figures gets this wrong often
enough that we spell it out rather than convert silently.
