Netflix publishes twenty places per country per week — ten films, ten series. It is a fixed pie, and
that makes it one of the few things about the Korean wave that can be counted rather than asserted.

We counted all of it: 93 countries with a complete record, 268 weeks, **498,480 places**. Korean
titles hold **39,612** of them.

## 7.6% of the world, and half of Korea

| | Korean share of top-10 places |
| --- | ---: |
| All 93 markets | **7.7%** |
| The median country | 4.7% |
| South Korea itself | **49.7%** in 2025 |

Korea's own figure is the one that surprises people first. Even at home, in the market where every
Korean release lands and where the audience needs no subtitles, **fewer than half of Netflix's
top-10 places hold a Korean title.**

Ten markets sit above 20%, and there is no mystery about which ones:

| Korean share | Markets | Which |
| ---: | ---: | --- |
| 20% and above | 10 | South Korea, Vietnam, Indonesia, Malaysia, Taiwan, Thailand, Hong Kong, the Philippines, Singapore, Japan |
| 10–20% | 11 | Bangladesh, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Peru, Bolivia, Nigeria and five more |
| 5–10% | 24 | |
| 3–5% | 17 | |
| 2–3% | 22 | |
| Under 2% | 9 | Latvia, Norway, Estonia, Ukraine, the United Kingdom, Denmark, Ireland, the United States, Iceland |

Every market in the top band is in East or Southeast Asia. The bottom band is northern Europe and
the English-speaking world.

## The global share has not moved

| Year | Weeks | Korean share |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 2022 | 52 | 7.6% |
| 2023 | 53 | 8.3% |
| 2024 | 52 | 7.1% |
| 2025 | 52 | 8.2% |

Four complete years, and the share ends where it started. If the Korean wave has been growing since
2022, it has not been growing into these places.

That flat line is also the least interesting thing on this page, because it is an average of 93
countries and it hides the only movement in the data.

## What the average hides

| Group | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 | 2025 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| South Korea | 51.2% | 58.3% | 52.0% | 53.3% |
| The nine Asian markets that were once above 20% | **30.5%** | 26.9% | 19.9% | **20.8%** |
| The five largest English-speaking markets | 1.9% | 1.4% | 2.0% | 2.0% |

The nine markets where Korean titles were strongest gave up **9.7 points in four years** — better
than a third of the share they held. The English-speaking markets did not pick it up; they are
where they were. The global average stayed flat because nine countries falling is a small thing
inside an average of 93.

## All nine fell. That is the finding, not the order.

| Market | 2022 | 2025 | Change |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Vietnam | 34.4% | 25.8% | −8.6 |
| Indonesia | 34.4% | 26.7% | −7.7 |
| Malaysia | 31.4% | 21.5% | −9.9 |
| Taiwan | 27.1% | 21.3% | −5.8 |
| Thailand | 30.8% | 20.3% | −10.5 |
| Hong Kong | 27.9% | 19.4% | −8.5 |
| Philippines | 27.9% | 20.6% | −7.3 |
| Singapore | 27.8% | 19.2% | −8.6 |
| Japan | 32.8% | 11.9% | −20.9 |

We are not putting these in an order of merit and the ranking is not the point. **The point is that
there is no exception.** One market falling is a market. Nine out of nine falling, across a range of
languages, incomes and neighbouring content industries, is the same thing happening to all of them.

## Fewer titles, not shorter runs

A share can fall two ways: the same titles hold fewer places each, or fewer titles get in at all.
These are different problems and the data separates them cleanly.

| Year | Korean share | Korean titles charting | Places per Korean title | All titles charting |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2022 | 30.5% | **166** | 17.2 | 1,127 |
| 2023 | 26.9% | 145 | 17.7 | 1,158 |
| 2024 | 19.9% | 102 | 18.3 | 1,248 |
| 2025 | 20.8% | **110** | 17.7 | 1,247 |

The number of distinct Korean titles reaching these charts fell from 166 to 110. What each one did
once it arrived barely changed — 17.2 places to 17.7. **A Korean title that charts in these markets
performs as well as it did in 2022. There are simply far fewer of them.**

Meanwhile the total number of distinct titles charting rose from 1,127 to 1,247. The lists got
busier and Korean titles took a smaller part of them.

## The control: Korea's own chart

There is an obvious way this whole page could be wrong. We identify Korean titles through Wikidata,
and Wikidata records a new title later than Netflix charts it. If our recognition were quietly
decaying, recent years would look emptier of Korean content whether or not anything had changed.

Korea's own chart tests that, because it is measured with exactly the same identification:

| | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 | 2025 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| South Korea | 49.8% | 54.3% | 51.2% | 49.7% |

It does not sag. A ruler that was losing recent Korean titles would shorten here first and hardest,
because this is the chart most densely packed with them. It ends within a tenth of a point of where
it started while the nine markets lost more than ten points, so whatever moved was not the ruler.

## The peaks are one title

| Week | Korean share | Korean places | Largest single title |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| 2025-07-06 | **16.1%** | 300 | Squid Game — 240 places, **80.0%** |
| 2025-01-05 | 15.2% | 283 | Squid Game — 186, 65.7% |
| 2024-12-29 | 15.2% | 282 | Squid Game — 186, 66.0% |
| 2024-11-10 | 2.0% | 37 | A Virtuous Business — 8, 21.6% |

The last row is the lowest week in the record. Across 93 countries and 1,860 published places that
week, Korean titles held 37.

The highest week in five years is four-fifths one show. That is worth holding next to the paragraph
above about title counts: the thing that moves the global figure is not a rising tide of Korean
content, it is whether one enormous title happens to be out.

## What we are not saying

We are not saying Korean content is in decline as an industry. Our own pages show
[content exports growing 63% in two years](/article/korea-exports-grew-viewing-did-not) over a
period when top-of-chart viewing did not move, and this is another measurement of the same
disagreement rather than a verdict about it.

We are also not offering a cause. Netflix's own commissioning, the growth of local production in
Thailand and Indonesia, and the simple fact that more titles now compete for the same twenty places
are all plausible, and this data separates none of them. What it does say is narrower and firmer:
in the markets where Korean titles held the most ground, they hold a third less of it than they did
in 2022, and that is true in every one of those markets.
