Ask a Korean friend what their **animal year** is and you will get an answer instantly. Ask what
their *saju* is and you may get a pause — because that is the bigger thing, and most people know it
is bigger than they can explain over coffee.

Here is the part outsiders usually miss: **these are two different things, and Koreans use both.**

## The number first

In a 2026 national survey of 1,200 people aged 13 to 69, **62.3% said they had had their fortune
read at least once.** There are **15,853 registered fortune-telling businesses** in the country.
That is not a fringe habit. It is closer to a normal errand — something done before a wedding,
before signing a lease, before naming a baby.

## The animal year is the one you already know

Twelve animals, one per year, on a repeating cycle. Born in 2000 you are a dragon; born in 2001, a
snake. It works roughly the way a Western star sign does — one label, shared with everyone born in
the same window, easy to say out loud. Which is why it travels. English-language threads about
Korean astrology are full of people calling out to each other: *"Where my dragons at?"* *"Anyone
else a rooster?"* Nobody is asking a question. They are finding each other.

⚠ One catch before you announce yours: **the Korean year does not start on 1 January.** It turns in
early February. If you were born in January, the animal you have been telling people is quite
possibly the previous year's.

## So we counted

We keep a panel of **1,355 Korean actors** who appear in the cast of titles that reached a Netflix
country chart — the same people we measure in
[how far an actor's titles travel](/actor-reach). **1,047 of them have a birth year we can turn into an animal sign** — the rest are
January and February births we deliberately left blank, and people whose birth date is not recorded.

If the animal year had nothing to do with becoming one of these actors, chance would put **87.3** in
each sign. Here is what is there:

|---|---:|---:|
| Rat | 89 | +1.7 |
| Ox | 82 | -5.3 |
| Tiger | 78 | -9.3 |
| Rabbit | 86 | -1.3 |
| Dragon | 90 | +2.7 |
| Snake | 76 | -11.3 |
| Horse | 77 | -10.3 |
| Goat | 91 | +3.7 |
| Monkey | 91 | +3.7 |
| Rooster | 92 | +4.7 |
| Dog | 103 | +15.7 |
| Pig | 92 | +4.7 |

**Chi-square 7.77, against a 5% threshold of 19.68.** The distribution is indistinguishable from
chance. Dog is the largest and snake the smallest, and neither gap is bigger than what random
scatter produces across twelve boxes. The full table, with the debut ages of the same panel, is on
[the animal years page](/zodiac).

That is the whole finding, and it is a negative one. **The year you were born does not pick out who
ends up on a chart.**

## What that does and does not mean

It does not mean the practice is fake, and we are not going to pretend a count of actors settles
something a count cannot reach. What it settles is narrow and worth having: if anyone tells you a
particular animal year is the lucky one for a screen career, that claim is testable and it does not
survive.

**Saju** is the other thing, and it is not what we tested. *Saju* (사주) means "four pillars": the
year, the month, the day and the hour you were born, read together. The animal year gives you one
thing you share with roughly one person in twelve. Saju gives you four, and the combination is close
to yours alone. A Korean fortune teller is not primarily interested in your dragon — they want the
day and the hour.

## One honest limit, and it is a big one

The fourth pillar is the **hour of birth**, and most people do not have it. Without it three pillars
still stand, carrying the year, the season and the day. What cannot be built is the fourth. Any
reading that claims full precision without knowing your birth time is claiming something it does not
have.

It is also why you will not see us publish "here is a celebrity's saju." **Public profiles carry
birth dates and almost never birth times.** We can tell you how a thousand Korean actors' animal
years are distributed. We cannot tell you any one of their charts, and we will not pretend
otherwise.

⚠ There is a smaller version of the same problem inside our own count. Wikidata records a
year-only birth date as **1 January**, which put 34 people on that single day — nine times the daily
average. Those are not January births; they are people whose day is unknown. We treat them as
unknown. Had we not, this article would have opened by telling you Korean actors are unusually often
born in January.

## Where to start, if you want to

Find your animal year, and check the February cutoff before you commit to it. That is the free,
social, low-stakes end of it.

If you want the four-pillar version, dig up your birth time first — a hospital record, a family
register, a parent's memory of "just before dinner." Without it you get three-quarters of the
picture, which is genuinely useful. But it is worth knowing which quarter is missing.
