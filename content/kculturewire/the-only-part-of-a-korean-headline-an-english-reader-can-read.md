An English-speaking reader who opens a Korean entertainment site can read almost nothing on the
page — except the parts printed in Latin letters. Those are the handholds: **BTS**, **MV**,
**GUCCI**, **kg**. So we counted them.

We keep a daily photograph of the front pages of four Korean outlets, and we have already used it
twice this week: once for [the age in the headline](/article/the-age-in-the-headline-is-one-desk-not-a-country),
once for [the money in it](/article/each-korean-desk-has-one-number-and-they-never-mix). This is the
same file read a third way — not what the numbers say, but **what alphabet the headline is in**.

## The K-culture desk prints four times as much Latin script

| Desk | Beat | Headline-days | With Latin script | Share | Without its own initials |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Star News | K-culture | 215 | 70 | **32.6%** | 30.2% |
| TenAsia | K-culture | 73 | 15 | 20.5% | 19.2% |
| Maeil Business | business | 696 | 109 | 15.7% | 14.2% |
| Dong-A Ilbo | general | 404 | 29 | **7.2%** | 7.2% |

Across all four, 223 of 1,388 headline-days carry at least one Latin word — about one in six.

The last column is there because a newspaper puts **its own name** in its own headlines. *MK* is
Maeil Business's own abbreviation and it is that desk's single most frequent Latin word, 31 times in
ten days. Taking each outlet's initials out of its own count moves every rate down and **changes no
part of the order**, which is the part the comparison actually rests on.

## The kinds are different, and that is the more useful finding

Latin script is not one thing. Splitting the words by letter case gives four columns, and the desks
do not fill them the same way:

| Desk | ALL CAPS | Initial Cap | lowercase | MiXed | Latin words |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Maeil Business | 147 | 0 | 2 | 1 | 150 |
| Star News | 75 | 3 | 10 | 9 | 97 |
| Dong-A Ilbo | 24 | 0 | 5 | 2 | 31 |
| TenAsia | 7 | 0 | 7 | 1 | 15 |

The business desk is **98 percent capitals**: AI, ETF, GDP, IPO, IRP, GW. Those are the vocabulary of
a market report, and an English reader who knows finance can follow a Korean business headline
surprisingly far on the acronyms alone.

The entertainment desks are the ones with lower-case and mixed-case words, and their most frequent
Latin words are not concepts but **things you can buy**: LG nine times, TV seven, KIA five, GUCCI
five. The word an English reader is most likely to meet on a Korean entertainment front page is a
brand, and usually a brand attached to a person.

The clearest single case is the one headline that kept its place for five days running, about a
Google search record for a Gucci campaign. Its readable part is three words — **'FACE OF GUCCI'** —
and those three words, printed five days in a row, are 15 of Star News's 97 Latin words. One
campaign was a sixth of a desk's entire English-legible output for ten days.

## The word that crosses the whole newsroom is not English at all

| Latin word | Times | Where it comes from |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| MK | 31 | Maeil Business's own initials |
| AI | 30 | every desk, mostly business |
| kg | 19 | 15 of the 19 are the two entertainment desks |
| LG | 10 | mostly Star News |
| ETF · GW · TV | 7 each | ETF and GW business, TV entertainment |

**kg** is the surprise. It is a unit, not a word, and it belongs to the entertainment desks: 8 uses
at Star News and 7 at TenAsia against 4 at the general paper and none at the business desk. Weight in
kilograms is an entertainment-desk number in Korea, the way a won amount
[is a business-desk number](/article/each-korean-desk-has-one-number-and-they-never-mix) and a quoted
age is a K-culture one. That is now the third habit we have found that lives at one desk and not the
others.

## What a publisher can do with this

If you write about Korean culture in English and you read Korean front pages for leads, the practical
consequence is that **the two K-culture desks are the ones you can partly read without Korean** —
one headline in three at Star News has a handhold in it — while the general paper is nearly opaque at
7 percent. And the handholds are brand names, which means a foreign-language reader skimming Korean
entertainment coverage is being steered, without meaning to be, toward the commercial stories.

We are not going to say that is good or bad. We counted 102 distinct Latin words in ten days, and the
count is what we have.

## What this measurement is not

Case is not meaning. *K-pop* is a genre and *Netflix* is a company, and our letter-case split puts
them in the same column — so we use the split to compare desks with each other, never to say what a
word is. We also count a headline again each day it stays up, which inflates repeat-heavy desks; the
distinct-headline count is 1,010 against 1,388 headline-days, and we print both rather than pick one.
And we photograph once a day, so a headline that came and went between two captures never entered
this count at all.
