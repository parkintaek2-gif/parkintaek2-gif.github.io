Every chart page we have published counts places. A Korean series at number one in Vietnam and a
Korean film at number ten in Belgium both count as one place, and 492,900 of those places across 93
markets is how we get the figure we keep repeating: **7.7% of the chart is Korean.**

That figure hides a question we had never asked. The places are not identical. A tenth of them are
number one. Are Korean titles as likely to be there as anywhere else?

Mostly, no.

Added up, though, the answer looks like nothing at all. Across all 93 markets Korean titles hold
**7.8%** of number-one places against **7.7%** of places overall, and 50 of 93 markets sit lower at
number one than across their chart. That is a coin toss. Anyone who asked this question once and
stopped there would publish a flat line and move on, and we nearly did.

It separates only when the markets are grouped by how much of the chart Korean titles hold to begin
with — and then it separates in two opposite directions at once, which is precisely why the total
comes out flat.

## The middle of the chart

Group the 93 markets by how much of their chart is Korean to begin with, and the number-one column
separates from the rest.

| Korean share of the market | Markets | #1 | #5 | #10 | All places | #1 minus all |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Under 5% | 49 | 2.9% | 2.9% | 3.2% | 2.8% | +0.1p |
| 5–10% | 23 | 5.5% | 8.6% | 8.1% | 7.8% | −2.3p |
| 10–20% | 11 | 9.6% | 13.3% | 9.9% | 11.6% | −2p |
| 20% and over | 10 | 35.3% | 26.4% | 20.4% | 26.7% | +8.6p |

The two middle rows are humps. Korean titles are least common at number one, most common around the
middle of the list, and taper again toward number ten. The bottom row is the opposite: a slope that
starts at its highest point and falls all the way down.

This is not an average concealing disagreement. On the 5–10% row, **22 of 23 markets** individually
sit lower at number one than across their chart. On the bottom row, **none of the ten do** — every
one of them is higher.

Films and series are separate top tens, and a pattern that only appeared when they were added
together would be worth nothing. Split apart, the 5–10% row is −0.6 points for films and −3.9 points
for series; the bottom row is +4.2 and +13.0. Different sizes, same direction, four times over.

## The objection, which is right three times out of four

Number one is a tenth as many places as the chart. One title that stays on top for a year can
manufacture any shape you like, so the first thing to check is whether that is all this is.

In three of the four bands, it is close to all it is.

| Korean share of the market | Distinct titles at #1 | Largest title's share | #1 share without the top three |
|---|---|---|---|
| Under 5% | 48 | 67.9% | 0.6% (all places: 2.8%) |
| 5–10% | 67 | 36.2% | 2.9% (7.8%) |
| 10–20% | 67 | 28.9% | 5.9% (11.6%) |
| 20% and over | 250 | 5.3% | 30.8% (26.7%) |

The largest title is the same one in all four rows. In the 49 markets where Korean titles hold under
5% of the chart, *Squid Game* accounts for more than two-thirds of every Korean week at number one,
and removing the three biggest titles drops that band's number-one share to **0.6%** — against 2.8%
of the chart overall. The +0.1p in the table above was not a Korean chart reaching number one. It
was one title.

The bottom row does not behave that way. The average market there saw **83.1 different Korean
titles** reach number one, the largest took 5.3% of those weeks, and taking out the top three leaves
30.8% — above the 26.7% those markets give Korean titles across the whole chart. Reaching number one
there is ordinary.

So the honest summary is narrower than the table first suggests, and more interesting: **in most of
the world, Korean titles get onto the chart far more easily than they get to the top of it, and the
exceptions are one title. In ten markets, they get to the top more easily than they get onto the
chart at all.**

## What a rank cannot tell you

A position is an outcome. These figures cannot say whether a title placed fourth because fewer
people watched it, because it opened against something enormous, because it arrived with less
promotion, or because a market's audience treats a foreign title as a second choice rather than a
first. Netflix publishes the position and, for some weeks, the hours — never the reason. Anyone
who tells you which of those is at work is telling you something this data does not contain, and
we are not going to be the ones who do it.

Two other limits are worth naming. The bands are cut by each market's own measured Korean share, so
they are not regions and should not be read as a map, even though the bottom row happens to contain
markets that are mostly in Asia. And a market on the bottom row is a market where Korean titles were
already a quarter of the chart, so "they own the top there" is a statement about a chart that was
never crowded out to begin with.

## Why we bothered

Because "7.7% of the chart" is the number we sell, and it turns out to mean two different things. In
Vietnam it means Korean titles are a routine first choice. In Poland it means they are a reliable
mid-chart presence and, once, *Squid Game*. Those are not the same business, and a buyer deciding
where to place a title should not be handed one number for both.
