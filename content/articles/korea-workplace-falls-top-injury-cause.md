Every year, Korea's Ministry of Employment and Labor publishes a full accounting of workplace injuries by cause — a legally mandated statistic, not a survey. We pulled the five most recent years directly from KOSIS to see not just how many workers got hurt, but specifically how.

## The obvious guess is wrong

![Korea's top 5 workplace injury causes in 2024, with 5-year change from 2020.](/charts/workplace-injury-causes.svg)

| Cause | 2024 cases | 2020 cases | 5-year change |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Falls / slips (same level) | 28,244 | 20,659 | **+36.7%** |
| Occupational disease | 26,998 | 15,996 | **+68.8%** |
| Falls from height | 14,655 | 14,406 | +1.7% |
| Caught in machinery | 12,941 | 12,894 | +0.4% |
| Struck by / against objects | 11,220 | 7,503 | +49.5% |

*If you pictured Korean workplace injuries as mostly industrial accidents — machinery, falls from scaffolding — the numbers say otherwise. The single largest cause, every year for five straight years, is simply falling on level ground.*

## Why would falls be the biggest category, not machinery?

This is the question the raw count doesn't answer, and it's worth sitting with. A few things this data does tell us: falls-from-height and machinery entrapment — the injury types that sound most like "industrial accidents" — have barely grown in five years (+1.7% and +0.4%). Whatever is driving the overall 31.7% rise in total injuries, it is concentrated almost entirely in same-level falls, occupational disease, and being struck by objects — the three fastest-growing categories, all up between 37% and 69%.

That pattern is consistent with a workforce spread across more service-sector and logistics-type jobs, where slips on wet or cluttered floors and repetitive-motion or exposure-related illness are more common risks than being caught in a press or crane. But this dataset alone can't confirm that — it counts causes, not industries-of-employment for each cause, and it doesn't record workplace age, staffing ratios, or which safety programmes were or weren't in place. That's the next question worth asking, and one we don't have the data in hand to answer yet.

## The one growing fastest is not the one in the lead — yet

Falls still lead by a margin of 1,246 cases in 2024. But occupational disease has grown nearly twice as fast (+68.8% vs +36.7%) over the same five years, and at that relative pace, it's the category to watch — not because it's the largest today, but because the gap is closing. This is exactly the kind of thing a single year's headline count won't show you: you need the trend, not just the level, to see where the real change is happening.

This is a measure of reported cases, not workplace conditions at any specific company, and it is **not investment advice**.
