Korea's trade surplus did not drift wider over the past year. It stepped up. Sum exports minus imports across all partners, month by month on the customs books, and the balance that ran near **$6.5bn in July 2025** was **$36.1bn in June 2026** — more than five times as large, with almost all of the climb packed into 2026.

![Line chart: Korea's monthly trade balance rose from about +$6.5bn in July 2025 to +$36.1bn in June 2026, with the steepest climb after January 2026.](/charts/trade-monthly-balance.svg)

The half-year totals make the shift plainest. In the **second half of 2025** the surplus added up to **$50.0bn** — about $8bn a month. In the **first half of 2026** it added up to **$137.7bn** — about $23bn a month. Same country, same customs desk, roughly triple the monthly surplus two quarters later. Over the full twelve months, exports were **$871bn**, imports **$683bn**, and the surplus **$188bn**.

## Where the extra surplus came from

A surplus that nearly triples invites the obvious question: with whom? Take each partner's bilateral balance in the first half of 2026 and subtract its balance in the second half of 2025. Five names account for **$78.4bn of the $87.7bn total swing — about nine-tenths of it.**

![Bar chart: change in Korea's bilateral trade balance by partner, first half 2026 versus second half 2025. United States +$26.7bn, Hong Kong +$19.1bn, China +$17.8bn, Vietnam +$9.8bn, Taiwan +$5.1bn.](/charts/trade-partner-swing.svg)

The **United States** alone widened by **$26.7bn**, its surplus running from $23bn in the earlier half to $50bn in the later one. **Hong Kong** added **$19.1bn** — though, as flagged below, much of the Hong Kong number is goods passing through on their way to the mainland. And **China**, Korea's largest single trading partner, **flipped from a $4.3bn deficit to a $13.5bn surplus** — a $17.8bn turn, the sort of sign-change that rarely happens over six months in so large a relationship. **Vietnam** (+$9.8bn) and **Taiwan** (+$5.1bn) round out the five.

Everything else barely moved. The largest deteriorations — the United Arab Emirates, Brazil, France — were each under $2.5bn, a rounding error against the partners above. The surplus did not broaden across the map; it concentrated.

## What the numbers are, and are not

Two cautions travel with a chart this steep. First, these are **customs figures, and preliminary ones** — monthly trade data gets revised, and June's export level is unusually high, so the last point on the line is the one to treat most gently. It should not be annualised into "$430bn a year." Second, the **Hong Kong** entry is not what it looks like: a large share is *entrepôt* trade — goods trans-shipped onward to mainland China — so the true China figure is understated and the Hong Kong figure overstated as final demand.

This is also **goods trade on a customs basis**, not Korea's current account: it leaves out services and income. And the "swing" is a plain arithmetic decomposition — later half minus earlier half — not a model of *why* each partner moved. What the data shows, cleanly and without a forecast, is a surplus that tripled per month inside a year, and a short list of partners who did nearly all of it. This is a reading of the customs record, **not investment advice**.
