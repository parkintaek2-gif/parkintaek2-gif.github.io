Korea publishes its trade figures in a public statistics table and then corrects that table in place. When a number changes, the old one disappears. The table records only the date of the latest change, not what it replaced.

So since 7 September we have taken a full copy of the table every few days. Comparing the copies from 13 October and 23 October shows what happened in between: on **16 October, 729 of the table's 2026 figures were rewritten.**

## Eight months touched in one day

Every changed figure carries the same last-changed date, 16 October 2026. The revision reached back to January. Nothing from late 2025 moved.

| Month (2026) | Figures changed | Change to the total trade balance |
|---|---|---|
| January | 73 | −$82.1 million |
| February | 66 | −$20.9 million |
| March | 78 | −$150.2 million |
| April | 70 | −$16.9 million |
| May | 59 | −$1.7 million |
| June | 93 | −$1.1 million |
| July | 119 | +$14.4 million |
| August | 171 | +$93.8 million |

The more recent the month, the more figures changed: August had 171, more than twice January's 73. The direction split in the middle of the year. Every month from January to June ended with a smaller surplus than before, and July and August with a larger one. Over the eight months together the surplus shrank by **$164.8 million.**

## Small at the top, large underneath

At the national level the changes are fractions of a percent. The biggest was March imports, raised from $60.75 billion to $60.90 billion, or 0.25%. That one line is most of the reason March's surplus fell by $150 million.

Underneath the total, by partner country, the corrections are far larger in proportion. Korea's March imports from Kuwait were raised by 31%, from $397 million to $521 million. August exports to Australia rose 3.2% and to Malaysia fell 1.9%. A reader using the partner figures from early October was working with numbers that have since moved by several percent.

## Why keep the old numbers

A revised figure is not a mistake; corrections are how official trade data gets more accurate. The difficulty is that this table keeps no history of them. A chart or model built on the figures as they stood on 13 October cannot be reproduced from the table today, and nothing on the page says it has changed.

That is why we keep the snapshots. The count above can only be made by someone who saved the earlier copy, and Korea's statistics service does not save it. Our view on how far 2026 trade levels can be compared with earlier years is [a separate question](/article/korea-2026-trade-surplus-data-artifact). This piece counts only what changed inside 2026, and when.
