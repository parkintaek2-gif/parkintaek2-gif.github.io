Here is a genuinely good-looking number: Korea's youth (15–29) unemployment rate [fell from a peak of 9.8% in 2016 to 6.1% in 2025](https://seoulmarkets.com/article/korea-youth-unemployment-fell-since-2016). A falling jobless rate reads as unambiguous good news. But an unemployment rate is a fraction, and this particular fraction can fall for two opposite reasons — one you would celebrate, one you would not.

## What the rate actually is

The unemployment rate is the number of unemployed divided by the labour force — and to count as "unemployed" you have to be actively looking for work. That definition has a quiet consequence: **the rate falls if jobless young people find jobs, and it also falls if they give up looking and drop out of the labour force entirely.** In the first case the denominator holds and the numerator shrinks; in the second the person simply stops being counted at all. Same direction on the chart, opposite meaning for the person.

A single rate cannot tell you which is happening. It reports a ratio, not a reason. So a fall from 9.8% to 6.1% is consistent with a cohort finding work — and equally consistent with a cohort that has stopped trying. The number alone does not adjudicate.

## Why the youth number is especially slippery

The youth rate is not a small version of the national one. It runs [about twice the all-ages rate — 6.1% against 2.8% in 2025](https://seoulmarkets.com/article/korea-youth-unemployment-fell-since-2016), a ratio of 2.18, and it moves further and faster than the national line. Young people are entering the labour market, studying, doing military service, or waiting for a specific kind of job, which means the boundary between "unemployed" and "not in the labour force" is crossed far more often for them than for prime-age workers. The more porous that boundary, the more a headline rate can move without the underlying situation moving the way the number implies.

## What we report, and what we won't

We report the published rate, because it is real and reproducible. What we will not do is tell you the fall is simply "good" — because the rate itself does not carry the one piece of information that would settle it: how many left the labour force versus how many found work. That split is not in the number, so we do not invent it. As always, we would rather show you the rate and name what it cannot separate than hand you a clean verdict the arithmetic does not support. **This is a statistic, not a conclusion.**

## The debate

So: when a youth jobless rate falls, is that young people doing better — or young people counted out? The rate looks the same either way, and it will not tell you which. **What do you think?** This is a talking point, not a verdict — argue it out below.
