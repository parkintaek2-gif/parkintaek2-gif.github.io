Two facts about a Korean sector's workforce turn out to be almost the same fact: how long its people stay, and how many of them are women. Put the main sectors on both axes and they line up along a single downward slope.

## The longer they stay, the fewer are women

Across the eight sectors large enough to measure, median tenure and median female share correlate at **−0.68**. At one end sit the industries that hold onto people — materials at 8.6 years, a median 13% women. At the other, the ones with shorter tenure and far more women — health care at 4.9 years and 40%, communication services at 4.7 years and 35%.

| Sector | Median tenure | Median female share |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Materials | 8.6y | 13% |
| Consumer discretionary | 7.1y | 30% |
| Consumer staples | 6.9y | 33% |
| Information technology | 6.5y | 21% |
| Industrials | 6.4y | 14% |
| Financials | 6.2y | 35% |
| Health care | 4.9y | 40% |
| Communication services | 4.7y | 35% |

## The same old line

None of this is a mystery, but the −0.68 puts a number on it. The sectors that keep people longest are Korea's older, heavier industries — chemicals, metals, machinery — built decades ago around a mostly male workforce, and they still are. The sectors that turn people over faster are the newer service and care industries, where women are close to half the staff.

So a single slope carries two of the workforce's oldest features at once. It says nothing about pay — a sector can be 40% women and still pay them less, and that is a different column. What it maps is where women work in Korea's listed economy, and it is the same map as where people leave soonest.
