Korea's reputation is for long hours. The reputation is earned, but the trend runs the other way: on the country's own establishment survey, the average worker put in **176.6 hours a month in 2011 and 153.8 in 2025** — a **12.9% fall** over fourteen years, and it happened almost year by year.

## Down every year, not in one jump

![Line chart: Korea's average monthly hours worked per worker by full calendar year, 2011 to 2025, falling steadily from about 177 to about 154.](/charts/korea-working-hours.svg)

| Year | Hours / month | Annualised |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 2011 | 176.6 | ~2,119 |
| 2015 | 171.5 | ~2,058 |
| 2019 | 163.1 | ~1,957 |
| 2022 | 158.7 | ~1,904 |
| 2025 | **153.8** | **~1,846** |

The line barely pauses. Each year from 2011 to 2025 sits at or below the year before it — there is no reversal, only a slowing near the end. In fourteen years the average month lost about **23 hours of work**, roughly three full working days a year that used to be worked and now are not.

## Why the number looks different from the one you may have seen

Two honest caveats sit around this figure, and both matter.

First, this is the **establishment survey** — hours reported by employers for workers at firms with a regular payroll, averaged per worker. It is not the OECD's annual-hours series, which uses a different scope and method and comes out lower. Both are real; they measure slightly different things. We quote the survey we can pull and recompute ourselves, and we do not splice the two together.

Second, the **monthly numbers are calendar-driven**. A short February with the Lunar New Year inside it reads near 132 hours; a long July reads near 168. That swing is the calendar, not a change in how hard anyone works. Comparing one month to the next mostly measures which holidays fell where. Full-year averages remove that, which is why every number here is an annual one.

What the annual series does say, cleanly, is this: **the amount of time a Korean worker spends working has fallen by about an eighth since 2011, steadily, and had not stopped falling as of 2025.**

## Fewer hours, flat pay

The hours came down. Pay, adjusted for inflation, did not keep climbing to match: real wages [rose for a decade and then went flat after 2021](/article/korea-real-wages-flat-since-2021). Where a worker lands on both counts depends on where they work — [bigger firms pay more at every step](/article/korea-bigger-firms-pay-more-every-step) — and on getting hired at all, where [youth unemployment has fallen from its mid-2010s peak](/article/korea-youth-unemployment-fell-since-2016) even as more young people step outside the count.
