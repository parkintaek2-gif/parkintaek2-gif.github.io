A stock rating looks like information: buy, hold, sell — a scale, a verdict, a signal. But a scale only carries information if it can actually point in more than one direction. In Korea, for practical purposes, it points one way.

We counted. Across **55,580 rated broker reports since 2014**, the neutral "hold" rating [fell from 9.7% to 3.3%](https://seoulmarkets.com/article/korea-analysts-stopped-saying-hold), and over the whole twelve years there were **just 89 sell calls.** Not 89 a year — 89 in total. When the negative end of a scale is that empty, the scale has quietly collapsed into a single value with decorations.

## What a one-sided scale can and can't tell you

If almost every rating is "buy," then "buy" is the baseline, not the message. The information — if there is any — has to hide in the parts that still vary: which stocks get covered at all, how a rare "hold" reads as a soft warning, how a price target moves even as the label stays put. The label itself, the thing most readers actually see, has been compressed to the point where it distinguishes almost nothing.

This is the same problem we keep finding in different clothes. A market-value league table [ranks companies by a bet, not their output](https://seoulmarkets.com/article/korea-market-cap-is-not-productivity). A pay ranking [buries the distribution inside one average](https://seoulmarkets.com/article/korea-pay-ranking-hides-the-distribution). A rating scale that never says sell buries the signal inside one word. In each case a single, confident-looking marker survives while the information it claims to carry has drained out of it.

## Why we won't turn it into a signal

We could rank brokers by how bullish they are, or build a "consensus buy" score. We won't, for the reason we keep repeating: the compression is exactly where the honesty leaks out. We would rather show you the distribution — how many buys, how few holds, how vanishingly few sells — and let you decide what a "buy" is worth in a market where sell is almost never said. **This is a statistic, not a recommendation.**

## The debate

So: in a market where analysts issue 89 sell calls in twelve years, does a "buy" rating carry information — or has it become the price of admission? A scale needs two ends to mean anything. **What do you think?** This is a talking point, not a verdict — argue it out below.
