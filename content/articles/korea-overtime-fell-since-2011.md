The long-hours reputation has a specific line inside it: **overtime**. On Korea's own establishment survey, the average regular worker put in **11.5 hours of overtime a month in 2011 and 8.1 in 2025** — a fall of nearly a third, and most of it landed in a few years in the middle.

## Down a third, fastest in the late 2010s

![Line chart: Korea's average monthly overtime hours per regular worker by full calendar year, 2011 to 2025, falling from about 11.5 to about 8.1, with the steepest drop between 2017 and 2020.](/charts/korea-overtime.svg)

| Year | Overtime / month | Annualised |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 2011 | 11.5 | ~138 |
| 2016 | 10.7 | ~128 |
| 2019 | 9.1 | ~109 |
| 2020 | 8.3 | ~100 |
| 2025 | **8.1** | **~97** |

The decline is not a straight slope. It was slow through the first half of the 2010s — 11.5 down to 10.7 by 2016 — then dropped faster: **10.1 hours in 2017 to 8.3 in 2020.** Since 2020 it has sat near eight hours and barely moved. So the change is real and large, but it is concentrated in the late-2010s, not spread evenly across the fourteen years.

## What we are not saying

It is tempting to draw a straight line from Korea's **52-hour workweek**, phased in from 2018, to the steepest part of this drop. The years do line up. But this table carries hours worked, not the reason they changed — a law, a slowing economy, a shift in which industries are hiring, and the spread of paid leave could each move the same line. We report the timing plainly and leave the cause to the reader, rather than assert one from a single series.

What the data does say, cleanly: **recorded overtime for a Korean regular worker is down about 30% from 2011, the fall was sharpest in the late 2010s, and it has held near eight hours a month since 2020.** It sits alongside the broader picture — [total hours worked have fallen](/article/korea-working-hours-fell-since-2011) too, while [real wages went flat after 2021](/article/korea-real-wages-flat-since-2021).
