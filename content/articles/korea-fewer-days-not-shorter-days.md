Korea's total working time has come down — [monthly hours fell about 13% since 2011](/article/korea-working-hours-fell-since-2011). The interesting part is *how*. It is not that the working day got shorter. It is that the month has fewer days in it.

## The day held; the days went

![Line chart: Korea's average number of days worked per worker per month by full calendar year, 2011 to 2025, falling from 21.2 to 19.1.](/charts/korea-workdays.svg)

Two numbers from the same survey, side by side:

| Year | Days / month | Hours / month | Hours per working day |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2011 | 21.2 | 176.6 | 8.33 |
| 2016 | 20.4 | 169.3 | 8.30 |
| 2019 | 20.0 | 163.1 | 8.15 |
| 2025 | **19.1** | **153.8** | **8.05** |

Days per month dropped from **21.2 to 19.1** — about **2.1 fewer working days every month**, a 9.9% fall. Total hours fell further, 12.9%. But divide one by the other and the length of an actual working day barely budges: **8.33 hours in 2011, 8.05 in 2025** — down just 3.4%.

## What that means for reading the trend

The headline "Koreans work less than they used to" is true, but the shape matters. Almost all of the decline is **fewer days on the calendar**, not shorter days at the desk. A Korean who works still puts in close to the same eight-and-a-bit hours on a working day that they did over a decade ago — they just do it on two fewer days a month.

We are not going to say from this table *why* the days fell — holidays, part-time share, paid leave and industry mix all sit behind it, and the survey carries the counts, not the reasons. What it does say cleanly is the composition: **Korea's shorter working month is a story of fewer days, not shorter ones.** It fits alongside the rest of the picture — [overtime fell faster still](/article/korea-overtime-fell-since-2011), while [real wages went flat after 2021](/article/korea-real-wages-flat-since-2021).
