The Korean stock market is, for the most part, a young market. The median listed company was incorporated **27 years ago**, and **39%** — 1,104 of the 2,819 that file a date — are under 25. Most of what trades on the exchange is a product of the last generation of the economy, not the last century. But at the far end of the distribution sit a few companies that were old before the country around them took its modern shape.

## A young market with an old spine

Sort the 2,819 companies by incorporation date and the tail thins slowly and then not at all. **525** companies are over 50 years old. **115** are over 70. And **9** are over 100 — founded, by the date on file, before 1926, which is to say before the **Republic of Korea** (1948) existed at all. These are not holding-company reshuffles with backdated paperwork; they are continuously operating businesses whose corporate life spans the colonial period, the war, and the whole of the country's industrialisation.

## Banks and drugmakers at the top

The two oldest by filed founding date are **Shinhan Bank** and **Dongwha Pharmaceutical**, each about **129 years** — a date around **1897**. **Woori Bank** follows at about 128. Below them the century club runs through **Kyungbang**, a textile maker from around 1919, and **Yuhan**, the pharmaceutical company founded about 1926 and still one of the country's most recognisable drug names. It is a telling list: finance and medicine, the two businesses that a modernising society needs first and keeps longest.

## A caveat that matters here

For the banks especially, the founding date is not quite what it looks like. A modern bank is usually the survivor of decades of mergers, and the date it files reaches back to the earliest predecessor in that chain — not to the entity trading under the name today. Shinhan Bank as a legal structure is far younger than 1897; the 1897 belongs to a bank it eventually absorbed. We report the date as filed, because that is the company's own account of its origin, but a reader should treat the oldest ages as the length of a corporate lineage rather than the age of one unbroken company.

## What this is, and what it is not

This is a count of years since incorporation, nothing more. Age is not a merit and youth is not a flaw; this is **not investment advice**, and nothing here says an old company or a new one is the better business or the better stock. Incorporation date is also not listing date — a 1926 company may have joined the market in 2015 — and it is not a footprint. What the numbers show, plainly, is the shape of the market's history: a large, young body of companies formed in the last three decades, standing on a thin spine of firms — mostly banks and drugmakers — that have been in business since before there was a South Korea to list them in.
