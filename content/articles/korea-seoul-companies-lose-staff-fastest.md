We have shown that Korean companies list overwhelmingly from the capital — 85.7% of head offices sit in Seoul, Gyeonggi or Incheon. Here is the other half of that map, and it points the opposite way. The region that holds almost all the companies is the region that holds onto its people the least.

## Six years in the capital, nine in the provinces

Take the 2,086 listed companies with 30 or more staff that report an average tenure, and split them by head-office region. In the **capital region** the median is **6.0 years**. Everywhere else it is **7.8** — nearly two years longer at the typical firm. The gap widens by city. The longest-tenured workforces are in the industrial and southern cities: **Gwangju** at a median **10.3 years**, **Busan** and **Ulsan** at **9.4**, **Daegu** at **9.3**. The shortest are in and around the capital — **Seoul 6.2**, **Gyeonggi 5.8**, and the young administrative city of **Sejong** at just **5.1**.

## The capital is a fast market, not a loose one

The pattern is not that provincial firms are kinder employers and Seoul ones are careless. It is that the two labour markets run at different speeds. The capital region is dense with employers, which means the next job is close: a worker who wants to move can, and many do, so tenure runs short even at good companies. A manufacturer in Ulsan or Gwangju sits in a thinner local market with fewer places to jump to, and its workforce skews older and more industrial to begin with — people arrive, settle, and stay. The same density that pulls companies and workers into the capital is what keeps nobody there very long.

## What this is, and what it is not

This is a map of reported tenure, not a ranking of employers, and it is **not investment advice** — a short average tenure is not a warning and a long one is not a badge. It also is not a clean natural experiment: provincial firms are older and more industrial, and that mix is part of why they retain, so the region and the industry stories are tangled together rather than separate. What survives is the shape: Korea concentrates its head offices in the capital and its loyalty in the provinces, and the further a company sits from Seoul, the longer, on average, its people stay.
